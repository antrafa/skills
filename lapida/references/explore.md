# EXPLORE — the idea and the problem behind it

Goal: know what hurts, for whom, how often, and what proves it — before any
solution is on the table.

## Required fields

| Field | Filled when |
|---|---|
| Original idea | the user's sentence, verbatim |
| Real problem | one sentence that names no solution, screen or technology |
| Target audience | a specific group you could go and find ("accountants at firms with 5–20 clients"), never "users" or "companies" |
| Job to be done | the job statement below, filled |
| Current evidence | who feels it, how often, what it costs today — each item tagged `strong` / `weak` / `none` |
| Current workaround | what the audience does today without the idea |

## Methods

**Jobs to Be Done** (Christensen; Ulwick's outcome-driven variant). People do not
buy products, they hire them to make progress in a situation. Fill:

> When `<situation>`, I want to `<motivation>`, so I can `<expected outcome>`.

A job statement that mentions the proposed product is not a job yet.

**5 Whys** (Toyota Production System). When the stated problem sounds like a
symptom or a solution ("we need a dashboard"), ask why until the answer stops
being about the tool. Usually 2–4 whys, not literally five; stop when the answer
is a cost, a risk or a lost outcome.

**The Mom Test** (Rob Fitzpatrick). Governs how evidence is weighed. Signals
that count: past behavior ("the last time this happened, what did you do?"),
money or time already spent on workarounds, a concrete commitment. Signals that
do not: compliments, hypothetical futures ("I would use it"), opinions about the
idea. If the user's evidence is of the second kind, tag it `weak` and say why.

Strength also depends on reach. `strong` is past behavior or money spent,
observed in several people of the audience. The author's own pain, however real
and measured, is one case: `weak`, and the reason is that nobody else has shown
it yet.

## Question repertoire

- "Take the product out of that sentence — what problem is left?"
- "Who exactly feels this? Could you name three of them?"
- "When did it last happen? What did they do then?"
- "What does it cost them today — time, money, risk — and how do you know?"
- "What do they use instead right now?" (the workaround is the real competitor)
- "Is this your own pain, someone you watched, or something you imagine?"

## Class adjustments

- `internal product`: the audience is a team or role; evidence is tickets,
  complaints, hours lost, incidents.
- `personal project`: the audience may be only the user; evidence is how often
  they hit the problem themselves. Keep the phase to 2–3 questions.
