# CHALLENGE — why this might not work

Goal: surface the assumptions nobody said out loud and find the one that, if
wrong, kills the idea. Skepticism here is a tool, not a posture: it ends by
saying which attacks the idea survived.

## Stance: devil's advocate

For this phase, play the professional skeptic. The job is to find, before
reality does, the reason this fails:

1. Rewrite the proposal in its most honest form — what it promises and what it
   assumes.
2. Attack the most fragile assumption with a concrete failure mechanism, never
   "this might not work".
3. Build the best argument against the idea, then test whether that argument
   holds up too.
4. Separate an **assumption** (testable) from a **bet** (not testable, so it
   needs a plan B), and "I disagree" from "this has a structural flaw".
5. State the cost of being wrong, whether it is reversible, and the asymmetry:
   how much is gained if it works, how much is lost if it fails.

Skepticism that never agrees is noise: close the phase saying which attacks the
idea survived. If the user or the invoking skill supplies its own critique lens,
apply it on top of these steps.

## Required fields

| Field | Filled when |
|---|---|
| Assumptions (DVF) | at least one per applicable lens, each with evidence strength |
| Riskiest assumption | one, chosen on the map below, with the reason |
| Pre-mortem | the most likely cause of failure, written as a past event |
| Doing nothing | what happens if the idea is never built, and who notices |
| Contradictions | tensions between answers so far, or `none found` |

## Methods

**Assumption Mapping** (David Bland, *Testing Business Ideas*; DVF lenses from
IDEO). Sort every assumption into:

- **Desirability** — do they want it? (problem, audience, willingness to change)
- **Viability** — does it pay off? (price, cost, channel, margin; for
  `internal product`: time saved vs. time to build and maintain)
- **Feasibility** — can it be built and run? (tech, skills, data, legal)

Then place each on two axes: **importance** (does the idea die if it is false?)
and **evidence** (strong / weak / none). The riskiest assumption is the most
important one with the weakest evidence. That one — not the most interesting
one — drives FIT's next test.

**Pre-mortem** (Gary Klein). "It is twelve months from now and this failed.
What happened?" Write the most likely story in the past tense, then check which
assumption it breaks. A pre-mortem that blames only "execution" has not found
the cause yet.

## Question repertoire

- "What has to be true for this to work, and what shows it is?"
- "It is a year from now and this failed — what is the most likely reason?"
- "If they keep using the current workaround forever, what do they lose?"
- "Which answer so far contradicts another?"
- "Is this an assumption you can test, or a bet that needs a plan B?"

## Class adjustments

- `business`: all three lenses are mandatory.
- `internal product`: viability is maintenance cost and adoption effort.
- `personal project`: desirability for the user and feasibility in their spare
  time; one assumption and one pre-mortem are enough.
